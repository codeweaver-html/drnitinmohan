India Reports Progress in Childhood Vaccination and Nutrition

India’s latest National Family Health Survey (NFHS-6), released in May 2026, reports encouraging improvements in childhood immunisation and nutrition. Full vaccination coverage among children aged 12–23 months increased from 83.8% to 87.1%. The survey also recorded…

Read article ↗

Nutritional indicators also showed progress. Stunting among children under five declined from 35.5% to 29.3%, while severe wasting decreased from 7.7% to 5.2%. These findings suggest improvements in several important dimensions of child health, although undernutrition remains a significant public health concern.

The results reinforce the importance of routine immunisation, breastfeeding support, appropriate complementary feeding and early identification of growth faltering. Healthcare professionals and families both have an essential role in ensuring that children receive timely preventive care.

However, national averages can conceal differences between states, districts and socioeconomic groups. Continued investment in primary healthcare, nutrition programmes and community-based follow-up will be necessary to sustain progress and reach vulnerable populations.

The key message is clear: improving child health requires not only access to treatment but also strong preventive healthcare from pregnancy through early childhood.

Typhoid Antibiotic Resistance in India: New Research Offers Important Insights

Published 21 October 2026 · 4:18 PM IST

Read article ↗

These findings demonstrate why antibiotic selection must be guided by current evidence rather than historical prescribing habits. A medicine that was effective in the past may no longer provide reliable treatment when bacterial resistance changes. Conversely, some older antibiotics may regain a role when laboratory testing confirms susceptibility.

For clinicians, blood culture and antimicrobial susceptibility testing can help guide appropriate treatment wherever feasible. Local resistance patterns are particularly important because bacterial susceptibility may vary across regions and healthcare settings.

Prevention remains equally essential. Typhoid vaccination, safe drinking water, sanitation, hand hygiene and food safety can reduce transmission and the need for antibiotic treatment.

The study does not mean that antibiotics should be changed routinely or that older drugs are suitable for every patient. Treatment decisions must remain individualised and follow current clinical guidance.

The broader lesson is that continuous resistance surveillance and responsible antibiotic use are essential to preserving effective treatments for future generations.

Childhood Scrub Typhus: Can Early Laboratory Markers Predict Severe Illness?

Scrub typhus remains an important cause of acute febrile illness in children across South Asia. A study published in October 2026 examined children admitted with confirmed scrub typhus to a tertiary-care centre in South India, investigating…

Published 15 October 2026 · 9:26 AM IST

Read article ↗

The researchers studied 181 children aged 1–14 years. Critical deterioration occurred in 34.3% of the cohort, using a composite outcome that included complications such as shock, acute respiratory distress syndrome and multiple organ dysfunction. Among the children who experienced critical deterioration, the reported case fatality rate was 12.9%.

Several admission findings were associated with worsening illness, including an elevated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, increased C-reactive protein, lower serum sodium and delays in starting doxycycline. Serum albumin also showed strong discriminatory performance in the study’s initial analyses.

These findings may help clinicians identify children who require closer monitoring and timely assessment for complications. Nevertheless, the study was retrospective and conducted at a single tertiary-care centre. Its predictive model requires external validation before routine clinical use.

Clinicians should not rely on any single laboratory marker to determine severity or treatment. Clinical examination, haemodynamic status, organ function and the overall pattern of illness remain essential.

The study highlights the importance of early recognition, appropriate antimicrobial treatment and careful monitoring of children with suspected severe scrub typhus.

Whooping Cough Worldwide: Why Pertussis Surveillance Still Matters

Pertussis, commonly known as whooping cough, continues to be an important vaccine-preventable respiratory infection worldwide. A global burden study published in October 2026 in Nature Medicine analysed pertussis estimates covering 1990–2023, providing an updated perspective on…

Published 8 October 2026 · 2:37 PM IST

Read article ↗

Pertussis is caused by Bordetella pertussis and spreads through respiratory droplets and close contact. Although infection can affect people of any age, infants are particularly vulnerable to severe complications, including pneumonia, apnoea and hospitalisation. Early symptoms may resemble a common cold before persistent coughing develops.

The findings reinforce the importance of maintaining high vaccination coverage and reliable disease surveillance. Routine childhood immunisation remains central to prevention, while vaccination during pregnancy, where recommended under national guidelines, can help protect young infants before they complete their own vaccine series.

Healthcare professionals should remain alert to suspected pertussis in infants with coughing episodes, breathing pauses or feeding difficulties, particularly when there is a history of exposure. Testing and treatment decisions should follow applicable clinical guidance.

Global estimates are valuable for identifying changes in disease burden and gaps in prevention, but they do not replace local surveillance data. Differences in testing, reporting and healthcare access can affect comparisons between countries.

The central message is that vaccine-preventable diseases require continued attention even when effective vaccines are available. High coverage, early recognition and timely public health action remain essential.

Source: Nature Medicine — Global burden of pertussis, 1990–2023 ↗

Artificial Intelligence in Paediatrics: Helpful Assistant or Clinical Risk?

Artificial intelligence is increasingly being explored as a tool for medical education, clinical decision support and patient communication. A comparative study published in 2026 in Pediatric Research evaluated the performance of large language models in paediatric…

Published 2 October 2026 · 11:05 AM IST

Read article ↗

Such research addresses an important question: can AI systems provide sufficiently accurate explanations of childhood infections to support learning and clinical reasoning? These tools can summarise medical information, generate practice questions and help learners organise complex topics. However, their ability to produce convincing explanations does not guarantee clinical accuracy.

Paediatrics presents particular challenges because diagnosis and treatment often depend on age, weight, developmental stage and rapidly changing clinical status. An incorrect medication dose or missed warning sign can have serious consequences. AI systems may also overlook unusual presentations, reproduce biases in training data or provide outdated recommendations.

For these reasons, AI should complement rather than replace established clinical resources, professional judgement and direct patient assessment. Medical students and doctors should verify recommendations against trusted guidelines, current evidence and local protocols.

Before AI tools are incorporated into clinical workflows, they require appropriate validation, privacy safeguards and monitoring for errors. Educational performance alone is not proof of improved patient outcomes.

The future of AI in paediatrics will depend not simply on more capable models, but on safe implementation and accountability. Used carefully, AI may support learning and efficiency while keeping clinicians responsible for decisions that affect children’s health.

Source: Pediatric Research — Large language models in paediatric infectious disease education ↗
